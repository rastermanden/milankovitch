Milankovitch cycles

Earth's orbit stretches, its axis nods and wobbles, over tens of thousands of years. Those slow rhythms change how much summer sunlight reaches the far north, and that sets the pace of the ice ages.

The hinge is northern summer sunlight

Milutin Milanković, working in Belgrade between the 1920s and 1940s, computed by hand how these orbital cycles alter the sunlight arriving at every latitude and season. His central insight was about where it matters. Ice sheets grow on the great northern continents, so the decisive quantity is midsummer sunlight around 65°N. Cool summers let the previous winter's snow survive; ice spreads and reflects more sunlight; the planet cools further. Hot summers melt the ice back.

In 1976 Hays, Imbrie and Shackleton found exactly these periods in deep-sea sediment cores, and the cycles went from hypothesis to “pacemaker of the ice ages”. This page computes the three cycles from the astronomical series of Berger (1978), evaluates the resulting sunlight, and feeds it into a deliberately simple ice–albedo model. Every number on the page is live.

Eccentricity · ~100,000 and ~400,000 years

How stretched is the orbit?

Earth's orbit is an ellipse with the Sun at one focus, not at the centre. Today it is nearly circular: Earth comes about 3% closer to the Sun in early January than in early July, which means roughly 7% more sunlight at perihelion. Pulled by Jupiter and Saturn, the ellipse stretches to e ≈ 0.058 and relaxes again. Eccentricity barely changes the sunlight over a whole year. Its real job is to set the volume of the precession cycle below: a rounder orbit mutes the contrast between seasons, a stretched one amplifies it.

Obliquity · ~41,000 years

How far does the axis lean?

Earth's axis is tilted 23.4° from the perpendicular to its orbit. That tilt is why there are seasons, and it nods between 22.1° and 24.5° every 41,000 years or so. More tilt means stronger seasons: the poles get more midsummer sun and the Arctic Circle creeps poleward. Less tilt means milder polar summers, snow that survives, and ice caps that creep toward the equator. Ice reflects far more sunlight than ocean or forest, so growing ice raises the planet's albedo, less energy is absorbed, and the cooling feeds on itself. Precession · ~19,000–23,000 years

Which season is closest to the Sun?

Like a spinning top, Earth's axis slowly traces a cone, once every 26,000 years. Combined with the slow turning of the ellipse itself, the climatic cycle repeats every 19,000–23,000 years. Precession does not change the tilt, only where it points, and therefore which season falls at perihelion. Today Earth is closest to the Sun in early January: northern winters are a little milder, northern summers a little cooler. Eleven thousand years ago it was the reverse, northern summer at perihelion, midsummer sunlight up to 8% stronger, and the last great ice sheets collapsed.

What the numbers are, and what they are not

  • Orbital elements. Eccentricity, obliquity and the longitude of perihelion are evaluated from the trigonometric series of A. Berger, Long-term variations of daily insolation and Quaternary climatic changes, J. Atmos. Sci. 35 (1978), truncated to the leading terms. Over the last million years the truncated series stays within a few percent of the full solution; for precise work use Laskar et al. (2004).
  • Sunlight. Daily-mean insolation at the top of the atmosphere uses the standard formula with a solar constant of 1361 W/m². “65°N midsummer sunlight” is the daily mean on the June solstice, the classic Milankovitch diagnostic.
  • Ice edges. A toy model: the equatorward edge of each polar ice cap moves linearly with that hemisphere's midsummer sunlight (0.34° of latitude per W/m² in the north, less in the south, which is anchored by Antarctica), starting from 70°N and 66°S today. Real ice sheets take thousands of years to respond and depend on land, ocean and CO₂; this page shows the equilibrium tendency, not the history.
  • Effective albedo. Ice-covered latitudes reflect 66% of sunlight, everything else 27%. The planetary value is the average weighted by each latitude's annual sunlight, so ice near the poles counts for less than it would by area, and a larger tilt, which brightens the poles, makes polar ice matter more. “Absorbed sunlight vs today” is the resulting change in the global-mean absorbed solar power; for scale, doubling CO₂ adds about 4 W/m².
  • Not a climate model. There is no ocean, no carbon cycle, no ice dynamics and no lag. Milankovitch forcing is the pacemaker; feedbacks turn its faint beat into ice ages.
